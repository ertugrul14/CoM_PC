Curbside Intensification — Foundation Fixes (Version 2 → 3)

Methodology stress-test, Category-A corrections · Decisions D-011 to D-015 · 3 June 2026 · Steps 01–08 changed under OVERRIDE; cube rebuilt; model retrain pending.

1. Why this round of changes

An internal stress-test of the pipeline found five foundation-level issues in how the raw data was prepared and fed to the model. None of them were visible in the headline accuracy number, but each one quietly distorted what the model was learning or what the results meant. The goal of this round was not to chase a better score — it was to make the numbers honest and defensible, so the thesis can stand up to examination. Three of the fixes deliberately make the model look slightly harder-working rather than better, because they remove a free pass it was previously getting.

3. Each change in detail

D-011Weather aligned to Melbourne local time

ProblemThe pedestrian and parking data are stamped in Melbourne local time, but weather was downloaded in UTC and merged on the clock label. Melbourne runs 11 hours ahead, so 5 pm foot-traffic was paired with roughly 6 am weather.
Why it mattersThe model was effectively told "it was cool and dark when the evening crowds appeared." That scrambles any real link between weather and behaviour — and indeed the audit found weather was hurting predictions, a classic symptom of misaligned signals.
FixFetch weather with the region's timezone so its clock matches the activity data.
BeforeWarmest hour of the day landed at the 4 am label (physically impossible)
AfterWarmest hour lands at 2 pm, coldest at dawn — a correct daily cycle

D-012Foot-traffic put on a log scale before normalisation

ProblemPedestrian counts are extremely lopsided: most streets are quiet, a few are very busy. A single average-and-scale pushed the busy streets into an extreme numeric range (4–10 standard deviations out), which neural networks handle badly.
Why it mattersThe model spent its effort wrestling with a handful of giant numbers instead of learning patterns evenly across the city. We chose a log1p transform (which gently compresses large counts) rather than scaling each street separately, because it fixes the range problem without erasing the real difference between a quiet lane and a major mall.
BeforeScaled foot-traffic spanned an unstable 0 to ~10
AfterNow a well-behaved −1.7 to 3.4 — the problematic tail is gone
NoteReported errors stay in real pedestrian units, so they remain comparable to the previous model. Parking occupancy was left untouched — it is already on a tidy 0–100% scale.

D-013Sensor outages no longer faked as zero

ProblemWhen a pedestrian sensor reported nothing, the gap was filled with zero — indistinguishable from a genuinely empty street. About one in ten sensor readings were these fake zeros.
Why it mattersThis is the most important fix for the thesis's core idea. The project looks for "flexibility windows" — times when a street is quiet enough to repurpose the kerb. A broken sensor filled with zeros manufactures a fake flexibility window, which could lead to a wrong policy recommendation. Now: brief dropouts (up to 1 hour) are smoothly interpolated, while longer outages are flagged and excluded from training and scoring rather than counted as "quiet."
Result6.3% of sensor readings identified as real outages and set aside; brief gaps repaired
Honesty noteThe model's reported error is now measured only on bins where the sensor was actually working. It may differ slightly from the old figure — not because the model changed, but because we stopped grading it on made-up data.

D-014Per-street confidence for guessed streets

ProblemOnly ~5% of streets have real sensors; the rest have their foot-traffic estimated. Previously every estimated street was stamped with the same confidence, regardless of how reliable that estimate actually was.
Why it mattersA reader (or the map) should be able to tell a well-grounded estimate from a shaky one. Each estimated street now borrows its confidence from the most similar measured streets (similar land use, size, activity).
OutcomeOnly 2 streets earned the high-confidence tier — and that is the honest answer. The underlying estimation quality is genuinely weak (it explains only ~18% of street-to-street variation), so the system now says so instead of implying false certainty. This reinforces the thesis framing: imputation buys city-wide coverage, not city-wide accuracy.

D-015Streets without parking sensors marked "unknown", not "empty"

Problem1,254 streets have no parking sensor. Their occupancy was set to 0%, which the model reads as "this kerb is reliably empty."
Why it matters"We didn't measure it" and "it's empty" are completely different claims. Treating the first as the second biases any scenario that weighs the cost of removing parking. These streets are now set to the average measured occupancy, which reads as a neutral "unknown" rather than a confident "empty." Parking predictions are still only reported for the 143 streets that actually have sensors.

4. What this means for the results

  • Accuracy: roughly unchanged in headline terms — these are correctness fixes, not score-chasing. Foot-traffic error should stay near its previous level (~5.5 pedestrians per 15 min), now measured on real data only.
  • Weather: should flip from harmful to genuinely useful in the model's feature ranking — the clearest testable sign the alignment fix worked.
  • Trust & honesty: the model now distinguishes measured from estimated, working sensors from broken ones, and "empty" from "unknown." Every limitation that was previously hidden in the data is now visible and labelled — which is exactly what an examiner looks for.
  • Scenarios: evening pedestrianisation costs are no longer understated, and intervention impacts are computed without the earlier hidden distortions.
