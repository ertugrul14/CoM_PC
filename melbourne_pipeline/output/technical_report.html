Ertugrul Akdemir
Melbourne CBD Urban Intelligence Research Group
Manuscript submitted for review — 2026

Abstract

Urban curbside space is among the most contested and economically significant resources within modern central business districts. As cities grapple with the competing demands of parking, pedestrianisation, outdoor dining, and goods delivery, evidence-based frameworks for reallocating this space remain scarce. This paper presents a complete end-to-end computational pipeline for Melbourne's Central Business District (CBD) that integrates multi-source sensor data, graph-based street network representation, spatio-temporal deep learning, and unsupervised street typology classification to support data-driven curbside reallocation decisions. The pipeline processes 149 days of 15-minute-resolution parking occupancy and pedestrian sensor readings across 1,397 arterial streets, complemented by land-use data from the Census of Land Use and Employment (CLUE) and meteorological inputs. A dual-branch Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) architecture combined with a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) temporal encoder and per-node learned bias achieves a test-set coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.927 for pedestrian flow prediction, substantially outperforming single-branch baselines. Unsupervised clustering using Gaussian Mixture Models on 84-dimensional temporal activity profiles, supplemented by five engineered behavioural scores, yields four stable and interpretable street archetypes (Silhouette = 0.651, Adjusted Rand Index = 0.964). Together, these outputs generate spatially explicit intervention opportunity maps that identify streets suitable for morning pedestrianisation, evening outdoor dining expansion, parking reallocation, and weekend leisure activation. The methodology is transferable to other instrumented urban centres and offers a replicable framework for evidence-based urban mobility planning.

Keywords: spatio-temporal graph neural networks; pedestrian flow prediction; curbside reallocation; urban mobility; graph convolutional network; Gaussian mixture model; Melbourne CBD; urban data science

1. Introduction

1.1 Background and Context

Urban curbside space — the strip of road immediately adjacent to the footpath — has historically been allocated to private vehicle parking. This allocation reflects planning assumptions from the mid-twentieth century that prioritised automobile access to commercial precincts. In contemporary high-density central business districts, however, this assumption is increasingly untenable. Pedestrian footfall has become the dominant driver of retail and hospitality revenue; shared mobility services, last-kilometre delivery fleets, and outdoor dining have emerged as legitimate competing uses; and evidence from both Europe and Australia suggests that pedestrianisation and outdoor seating materially increase economic activation per square metre compared to equivalent parking provision.1

Melbourne's CBD exemplifies these tensions. The city's Hoddle Grid — a dense network of laneways, arterial streets, and mixed-use corridors — generates foot traffic patterns that are highly heterogeneous across space and time. A street serving a morning commuter precinct behaves very differently from one adjacent to a late-night hospitality cluster, yet both may carry identical parking allocation under current policy. The City of Melbourne has committed, through its Transport Strategy 2030 and various local precinct plans, to progressively reallocating curbside space toward higher-value uses; however, such decisions have predominantly been made on the basis of aggregate pedestrian counts and qualitative local knowledge rather than granular, evidence-grounded modelling.2

1.2 Problem Statement and Research Gap

Two interlocking challenges limit evidence-based curbside reallocation. First, the city's pedestrian sensor network, while extensive, covers only 68 of 1,397 arterial streets directly; the remainder must be inferred. Second, even where pedestrian data exist, the temporal and spatial heterogeneity of street activity is rarely captured in a form that enables systematic comparison across the network. Existing approaches either rely on static count-based surveys — which are expensive, infrequent, and spatially sparse — or apply coarse zoning classifications that mask within-zone variation.

Graph neural networks have demonstrated strong performance in urban traffic and pedestrian prediction tasks by explicitly modelling the relational structure of street networks.3 However, most published architectures treat spatial proximity as the only meaningful relational signal. Streets that share functional characteristics — similar land-use composition, employment density, and hospitality mix — influence one another's pedestrian dynamics in ways that pure proximity does not capture. A dual-graph formulation that jointly models topographic adjacency and semantic (land-use) similarity has not, to the authors' knowledge, been applied to curbside reallocation opportunity scoring at the street segment level.

1.3 Research Aims and Contributions

This paper describes and evaluates a complete pipeline — from raw sensor ingestion to spatially explicit intervention maps — that addresses both challenges. The specific contributions are:

(1) A dual-branch GCN–GRU architecture combining spatial proximity graphs and semantic land-use similarity graphs, augmented with per-node learned bias terms, that achieves test R² = 0.927 for 15-minute-ahead pedestrian flow prediction across 1,397 streets.

(2) An XGBoost-based pedestrian imputation approach that extends sensor coverage from 68 observed streets to the full 1,397-street network, using spatial lag features derived from the proximity graph.

(3) A temporal aggregation and Gaussian Mixture Model clustering procedure that identifies four stable street archetypes from 84-dimensional weekly activity profiles, supplemented by five interpretable behavioural score dimensions.

(4) An intervention opportunity scoring module that translates model outputs and street archetypes into actionable curbside reallocation recommendations at the individual street segment level.

2. Context and Conceptual Foundation

2.1 Study Area

The study area is the Melbourne Central Business District, bounded approximately by the Yarra River to the south, Spring Street to the east, La Trobe Street to the north, and Spencer Street to the west. This area encompasses the Hoddle Grid, major retail precincts (Bourke Street, Swanston Street), the financial core (Collins Street), and a high-density hospitality and entertainment zone. It is among the highest-footfall urban precincts in Australia, with pre-pandemic pedestrian counts exceeding 100,000 persons per hour on peak blocks during peak times.4

The street network is represented as a polygon layer of 1,397 eligible arterial and council major streets, filtered from approximately 3,975 total CBD street segments. Each street polygon contains centroid coordinates used for spatial graph construction and serves as the fundamental unit of analysis. Streets classified as intersections are excluded to avoid double-counting at junction points.

2.2 Key Terminology

Several domain-specific and technical terms recur throughout this paper. Pedestrian flow (ped_flow) denotes the count of pedestrian movements recorded by an automated sensor within a 15-minute interval; it is the primary prediction target. Parking occupancy rate is the proportion of parking bays on a street that are occupied at a given 15-minute interval, derived from event-level parking sensor records. Time bin refers to a specific 15-minute interval within the study window; there are 14,400 such bins over 149 days. A street profile is a vector of aggregated temporal features — mean pedestrian flow and parking occupancy across each of 42 day-of-week/time-block combinations — representing the behavioural signature of a single street. An archetype is a cluster label assigned to a group of streets that share a characteristic temporal activity pattern, and which implies a corresponding type of curbside intervention that would likely generate the greatest benefit for that group.

2.3 Curbside Reallocation as an Urban Decision Problem

Curbside reallocation refers to the administrative and physical conversion of on-street parking to an alternative use — footpath widening, protected cycling lanes, outdoor dining platforms, loading zones, or pedestrian plazas. Such decisions involve multi-stakeholder trade-offs among economic activation, traffic flow, disability access, and community amenity. The decision problem this research addresses is: given a street segment, which type of reallocation (if any) would produce the greatest net benefit, and during which time periods is that intervention most appropriate?

This framing differs from conventional traffic engineering in two important respects. First, the outcome variable is pedestrian welfare (captured as flow) rather than vehicle throughput. Second, the street typology is derived empirically from observed behavioural data rather than assumed from zoning classifications, enabling finer-grained targeting of interventions.

3. Related Work

3.1 Graph Neural Networks for Urban Flow Prediction

The application of graph neural networks to traffic and pedestrian forecasting has grown rapidly since the publication of the Diffusion Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network (DCRNN) by Li et al.5 DCRNN encodes road network topology as a directed diffusion process over a graph, enabling spatial dependencies to propagate bidirectionally through the network. Subsequent architectures — STGCN (Yu et al.),6 AGCRN (Bai et al.),7 and Graph WaveNet (Wu et al.)8 — have progressively integrated adaptive graph learning, gated convolutional temporal encoders, and dilated causal convolutions. These approaches consistently outperform purely temporal baselines (ARIMA, LSTM) on traffic prediction benchmarks.

Pedestrian-specific prediction is less studied than vehicle traffic prediction. Existing work tends to focus on crowd density estimation from camera feeds or social force simulation, rather than sensor-network-based forecasting at the street segment level. The closest methodological precursors are studies of subway station pedestrian volumes (e.g., Su et al.)9 and retail precinct foot-traffic modelling (e.g., Gong et al.),10 but neither applies dual-branch graph architectures to CBD-scale curbside analysis.

3.2 Street Typology and Urban Clustering

Unsupervised classification of urban street segments has been explored through various lenses: morphological (street width, connectivity), functional (land-use mix), and temporal (activity rhythm). Hao et al.11 use k-means clustering on temporally aggregated mobile phone data to derive activity archetypes for Shanghai blocks. Zhao and Zhang12 apply spectral clustering to GPS trajectory density surfaces for Beijing ring roads. Both studies demonstrate that temporal activity profiles are more discriminating than static features alone.

Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) have seen limited but growing use in urban street classification because they provide soft probabilistic assignments and naturally accommodate elliptical cluster shapes that are common in activity-profile feature space. The use of Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) for GMM model selection is well established in the statistical clustering literature13 and is adopted here.

3.3 Curbside and Pedestrianisation Planning

Empirical research on the economic and mobility effects of curbside reallocation is largely concentrated in European cities. Gehl and Svarre's observational work14 established that footpath widening and seating provision consistently increase dwell time and spending in commercial precincts. More recently, Litman15 provided a comprehensive cost-benefit framework for parking versus active use trade-offs, while Cervero et al.16 demonstrated significant pedestrian flow increases following parking removal in San Francisco's SoMa district. Australian evidence remains sparse, although the City of Melbourne's own before-after studies of laneway and street activation projects support broadly similar conclusions.

This paper advances the literature by coupling quantitative spatio-temporal modelling with typology-driven intervention scoring, enabling the prioritisation of reallocation candidates across an entire CBD street network rather than individual case studies.

4. Data and Study Setting

4.1 Study Period and Temporal Resolution

Data were collected for 149 days from 1 November 2025 to 30 March 2026 (UTC), encompassing the Melbourne summer season. All time series are discretised into 15-minute bins, yielding T = 14,400 timesteps. This resolution balances granularity (capturing intra-hour pedestrian dynamics) against data volume and model memory constraints. The summer window captures university holiday periods, the Christmas–New Year retail peak, and the Australia Day public holiday, providing a behaviorally varied training corpus.

4.2 Parking Sensor Data

Parking occupancy data were sourced from the City of Melbourne's IoT sensor network via a Supabase backend, comprising approximately 2.4 million raw parking event records across 1,981 individual bay sensors. Each record encodes an event type (Present or Unoccupied), a timestamp, and a sensor identifier. Occupancy rates are reconstructed by pairing Present and Unoccupied events with a two-hour maximum duration cap to prevent erroneous long-duration artefacts from unpaired sensors. The resulting time series reports the fraction of occupied bays per street segment per 15-minute interval. Of the 1,397 arterial streets in the study area, 138 have at least one parking sensor.

4.3 Pedestrian Sensor Data

Pedestrian counts are collected from 99 automated pedestrian counting sensors installed at key footpath locations across the CBD. These sensors — primarily infrared beam-break or stereo-vision units — record hourly counts that are disaggregated to 15-minute resolution using proportional allocation. Of the 1,397 study streets, 68 lie within the catchment of at least one pedestrian sensor. Table 1 summarises the key data dimensions.

4.4 Land-Use and Static Features

The Census of Land Use and Employment (CLUE) is the City of Melbourne's annual comprehensive audit of business activity, employment, and land use within the municipality. Eleven CLUE dataset layers were spatially joined to the street polygon layer, yielding nine static per-street features: total_jobs (employment density), cafe_count, cafe_total_seats, bar_count, bar_patron_capacity, business_count, poi_total (points of interest), dining_capacity (total restaurant seats), and area_m2 (street polygon area). These features capture the land-use character of each street and serve as both static model inputs and semantic graph construction signals.

4.5 Feature Engineering: Temporal and Cyclic Encodings

Four cyclic temporal features are constructed to represent the periodic structure of pedestrian activity. Hour-of-day is encoded as sin(2πh/24) and cos(2πh/24); day-of-week as sin(2πd/7) and cos(2πd/7). These trigonometric encodings ensure that temporal adjacency is preserved in feature space — for instance, 23:45 and 00:15 are represented as similar, as are Sunday and Monday — without requiring the model to learn ordinal adjacency implicitly. Binary flags for weekends, Victorian public holidays, and school holiday periods complete the temporal feature set.

4.6 Data Quality Considerations

Parking sensor data exhibit several quality issues typical of IoT deployments: unpaired events (a Present without a subsequent Unoccupied), sensor dropouts, and erroneous long-duration occupancies. A two-hour maximum duration cap addresses the last of these. Streets without parking sensors receive occupancy_rate = 0.0 throughout, which introduces a systematic zero-imputation bias for those streets; this is flagged via the valid_parking indicator in downstream outputs.

The pedestrian sensor network covers only 4.9% of study streets directly (68 of 1,397). The remaining 95.1% of streets receive imputed pedestrian flow values generated by an XGBoost gradient boosting model trained on the observed streets (see Section 5.3). All imputed values carry a ped_confidence of 0.5, compared to 1.0 for observed streets, enabling downstream components to weight or discount imputed values as appropriate.

5.2 Graph Construction (Step 4)

5.2.1 Spatial Proximity Graph

The spatial graph encodes geographic adjacency among streets. For each street centroid, the eight nearest centroids (k = 8) are identified using Euclidean distance, yielding directed edges. Edge weights are assigned via exponential decay:

wij = exp(−dij / σ) (1)

where dij is the centroid-to-centroid distance in metres and σ is set to the median pairwise distance within the k-neighbourhood. This ensures that proximate streets contribute strongly to their neighbours' representations while distant streets are effectively excluded. Exponential decay is preferred over inverse-distance weighting because it produces a smoother gradient and avoids the singularity at d = 0.

Physically, the spatial graph captures the fact that adjacent streets share pedestrian demand sources — a commuter or tourist walking through a precinct will pass through multiple consecutive streets, creating correlated pedestrian flows. If one street experiences elevated activity, its immediate neighbours are likely to as well.

5.2.2 Semantic Similarity Graph

The semantic graph connects streets with similar land-use compositions, regardless of spatial proximity. Seven activity features are used: total_jobs, cafe_count, cafe_total_seats, bar_count, bar_patron_capacity, business_count, and poi_total. An edge is created between streets i and j if at least two of these features are both active (non-zero) in both streets, the fraction of shared active features meets a minimum ratio threshold (60%), and the log-ratio similarity of shared features is below a threshold of 0.70:

simij = meank∈S |log(xik/xjk)|, edge if simij ≤ 0.70 (2)

where S is the set of features active in both streets and xik is the normalised value of feature k for street i. The semantic graph captures functional homogeneity: a hospitality-intensive laneway street and a similarly characterised street on the other side of the CBD may have correlated pedestrian dynamics during evening hours even though they are not spatially adjacent.

5.3 Pedestrian Flow Imputation (Step 5)

Because only 68 of 1,397 streets have direct pedestrian sensor coverage, a supervised imputation model is necessary to extend coverage to the full street network. An XGBoost gradient boosting model is trained using GroupKFold cross-validation with five folds, where the grouping variable is the street identifier. This ensures that streets are held out entirely in their assigned test fold, testing the model's cross-street (rather than within-street) generalisation ability.

The imputation target is the log1p-transformed pedestrian flow, addressing the right-skewed distribution of raw counts. Input features include all F = 23 features plus a spatial lag feature: the mean pedestrian flow of the k = 8 nearest neighbours in the spatial graph at the same timestep. Interaction terms between cyclic time encodings and the weekend indicator are also included. The log1p transformation (log(1 + x)) is applied because it stabilises variance and prevents the model from being dominated by a small number of very high-count streets; the inverse transformation exp(x) − 1 is applied to recover counts.

5.4 Temporal Profile Aggregation (Step 6)

To characterise each street's behavioural signature for clustering purposes, 15-minute time series are aggregated into weekly profiles. The day is partitioned into six time blocks: night (00:00–05:59), morning (06:00–09:59), work_am (10:00–11:59), midday (12:00–13:59), work_pm (14:00–17:59), and evening (18:00–23:59). These blocks reflect the natural rhythm of Melbourne CBD activity — commute peaks, retail trading hours, and hospitality periods. Over seven days and six blocks, each signal (pedestrian flow and parking occupancy) yields 42 features per street, for a 84-dimensional profile vector.

Five engineered behavioural scores supplement the raw profile vectors. score_morning is the mean pedestrian flow across weekday morning and work_am blocks; score_midday averages weekday midday and work_pm blocks; score_evening averages all-day evening blocks; score_weekend_ratio is the ratio of mean weekend flow to mean weekday flow (clipped to [0, 5]), capturing relative weekend activation; and score_parking_flex is the fraction of time blocks in which parking occupancy falls below 30%, indicating available physical capacity for conversion. These scores provide explicit axes that enable the clustering algorithm to distinguish streets more reliably than from the 84-dimensional raw profile alone.

A critical design decision was to not normalise pedestrian profile vectors by each street's overall mean flow (row-normalisation). Initial experiments with row-normalisation produced a Silhouette score of 0.083 — near random — because dividing each street's profile by its own mean destroys the between-street variance in absolute activity level that is the primary discriminant for curbside intervention planning. A high-footfall entertainment street and a low-footfall residential street may have similar normalised profiles despite requiring entirely different interventions. Retaining raw flow values and applying global z-score standardisation in the clustering step preserves this information.

5.5 Street Clustering (Step 7)

Prior to clustering, all 89 profile features (84 temporal + 5 scores) are standardised using z-score normalisation (zero mean, unit variance). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is then applied to reduce dimensionality to 20 components, which collectively explain 99.39% of total variance. The PCA step is applied for two reasons: first, it removes collinearity among time-block features (e.g., Tuesday morning and Wednesday morning are strongly correlated); second, it reduces the feature space to a compact representation that improves GMM convergence.

A Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) is fitted for k values from 2 to 10. For each k, the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) is computed; the optimal k minimises BIC. The BIC penalises model complexity (number of free parameters) against goodness of fit (log-likelihood), selecting the model that best explains the data without over-fitting. The optimal k = 4 achieves BIC = −64,705.1, compared to BIC = +7,043.2 with row-normalised profiles under the same procedure.

Cluster stability is assessed via bootstrap ARI (Adjusted Rand Index) over 20 re-runs. The ARI measures the agreement between two clustering assignments, corrected for chance; a value near 1.0 indicates near-identical cluster membership across runs. The mean ARI of 0.9643 confirms high stability, meaning that the four-cluster solution is robust to random initialisation.

Archetype labels are assigned based on two dimensions: overall activity level (mean pedestrian flow z-score across all time blocks) and parking flexibility (score_parking_flex). Streets with activity z-score greater than 1.0 standard deviation above the mean are labelled major_pedestrian_corridors. Streets with activity z-score below −0.5 are labelled latent_activation_potential, indicating underutilised streets with scope for demand generation. Among medium-activity streets, those with parking flexibility below 85% are labelled parking_reallocation_priority, and the remainder are labelled midday_retail_activation.

5.6 Data Cube Assembly (Step 8)

The model input is a three-dimensional tensor of shape N × T × F = 1,397 × 14,400 × 23, referred to as the data cube. Fifteen continuous features (all features except binary flags and the cyclic encodings) are standardised using global z-scores computed over the training period only, preventing information leakage from the validation or test periods. The sparse adjacency matrices for both graphs are symmetrically normalised using the Chebyshev renormalisation trick:

Â = D̃−1/2 Ã D̃−1/2 (3)

where Ã = A + I is the adjacency matrix with self-loops added and D̃ is the corresponding degree matrix. This normalisation ensures that node representations are weighted averages of their neighbours (rather than sums), stabilising gradient flow during training.

5.7 Spatio-Temporal Model Architecture (Step 9)

The prediction model — referred to as MultiGCN — is a dual-branch spatio-temporal graph neural network. Its architecture is illustrated in Figure 2. The model takes as input a window of W = 96 consecutive 15-minute timesteps (spanning 24 hours) for all N streets simultaneously, and outputs a single-step prediction of pedestrian flow at t+1 for each street.

5.7.1 Graph Convolutional Branches

Each GCN branch applies a single graph convolution to the F-dimensional input features, projecting them to a H = 64-dimensional hidden representation:

H = ReLU(Â · X · Wgcn) (4)

where X ∈ ℝN×F is the node feature matrix at a single timestep, Wgcn ∈ ℝF×H is the learnable weight matrix, and Â is the normalised adjacency as defined in Equation (3). The multiplication ÂX computes for each node a weighted average of its neighbourhood's features, enabling the model to incorporate spatial context. The two branches use identical architectures but separate weight matrices and separate adjacency matrices (spatial vs. semantic). Their outputs are concatenated to form a 2H = 128-dimensional representation at each (street, timestep) position.

The practical implication is that each street's representation encodes not only its own features but also a summary of what its geographic neighbours and its functionally similar counterparts are doing. A street seeing unusual pedestrian activity because of a nearby event will propagate that signal to adjacent streets via the spatial branch.

5.7.2 GRU Temporal Encoder

The Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) is a recurrent neural network architecture that maintains a hidden state across timesteps using learned gating mechanisms. Unlike a standard recurrent network, the GRU's update and reset gates allow it to selectively remember or forget prior context, making it well-suited to time series with varying temporal dependencies. A two-layer GRU with hidden_size = 64 and dropout = 0.1 between layers is applied to the W = 96 timestep sequence for each street independently. Only the final hidden state (representing the end of the 24-hour input window) is passed to the prediction head.

In practical terms, the GRU enables the model to recognise patterns such as "this street had elevated activity in the morning rush, so it is likely to show elevated activity at lunchtime" — temporal context that a purely spatial model cannot capture. The two-layer design allows the first layer to capture short-range temporal dependencies (e.g., within an hour) and the second to capture longer-range patterns (e.g., morning-to-afternoon correlation).

5.7.3 Per-Node Bias

The prediction head applies a linear transformation from H = 64 to a scalar prediction and adds a per-node learned bias term bi ∈ ℝ for each street i. The bias is initialised to zero and learned jointly with all other model parameters. This allows the model to correct for systematic over- or under-prediction on specific streets — for instance, a very high-footfall shopping corridor where the global model consistently under-predicts, or a peripheral street where it consistently over-predicts. Without this term, such street-level biases would need to be absorbed by the GCN or GRU layers, reducing their generalisation capacity.

The addition of per-node bias terms increased validation R² from approximately 0.88 to 0.935 in ablation experiments, representing the single most impactful architectural modification during development.

6. Experimental Setup

6.1 Training Configuration

The model is trained on a CPU-only environment (Intel-based workstation, no GPU acceleration). A chronological 70/15/15 split is applied: the first 70% of timesteps (bins 0–10,079, corresponding to 1 November 2025–13 February 2026) constitute the training set; the following 15% (bins 10,080–12,239, 14 February–8 March 2026) form the validation set; and the final 15% (bins 12,240–14,399, 9–30 March 2026) are held out as the test set. This chronological partitioning prevents any form of temporal leakage from future observations into the training distribution.

At each training iteration, a mini-batch of B = 8 non-overlapping windows of length W = 96 is randomly sampled from the training range. A total of 256 gradient steps are performed per epoch. The Adam optimiser (Kingma and Ba)17 is used with learning rate η = 10−3; a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler halves the learning rate when validation MAE fails to improve for 8 consecutive epochs. Gradient clipping at norm 1.0 prevents destabilising gradient explosions. Training stops after a maximum of 200 epochs or when validation MAE fails to improve for 25 consecutive epochs (early stopping).

6.2 Validation Protocol

To ensure stable early stopping, validation is performed on a fixed set of 128 evenly-spaced window starts within the validation range, reused identically every epoch. This design eliminates the ±3 MAE epoch-to-epoch noise observed with randomly sampled validation windows, enabling reliable convergence detection. Test evaluation uses a different set of 128 fixed windows within the held-out test range and is performed only once, after training completes, to preserve the integrity of the test set.

6.3 Baselines and Evaluation Metrics

Three evaluation metrics are reported. Mean Absolute Error (MAE) measures the average absolute difference between predicted and actual pedestrian counts per 15-minute interval per street; a lower MAE directly translates to more reliable operational predictions. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) penalises large errors more heavily than MAE and is sensitive to peak-period prediction failures. The coefficient of determination R² represents the proportion of variance in the observed pedestrian flow that the model explains; a value closer to 1.0 indicates a better fit.

The three metrics are defined formally:

MAE = (1/n) Σ |ŷi − yi| (5)
RMSE = √[(1/n) Σ (ŷi − yi)²] (6)
R² = 1 − [Σ(ŷi − yi)²] / [Σ(yi − ȳ)²] (7)

In the context of pedestrian flow prediction, a MAE of 4 pedestrians per 15-minute interval can be interpreted as an average street-level prediction error of approximately 16 people per hour — acceptable for curbside planning purposes, where decisions operate at the level of typical daily or weekly flow rather than individual intervals.

7. Results

7.1 Training Convergence

The model was trained for 194 epochs before early stopping was triggered. Figure 3 shows the validation MAE trajectory across training. 7.4 Graph Branch Contribution

To assess the independent contribution of each graph branch, ablation experiments were conducted by removing each branch entirely (replacing its output with zeros). Removing the spatial GCN branch increased validation MAE by +11.87 (a 2.95× increase over the baseline of 4.02), while removing the semantic branch increased MAE by +8.13 (2.02×). Both branches are essential; the spatial branch contributes more, consistent with the observation that geographic proximity is the dominant signal in urban pedestrian propagation. However, the semantic branch's substantial contribution (+8.13) validates the hypothesis that land-use functional similarity provides non-redundant predictive information.

8. Discussion

8.1 Model Performance in Context

A test R² of 0.927 is high relative to the published spatio-temporal forecasting literature. DCRNN reported R² values of approximately 0.82–0.90 on METR-LA and PEMS-BAY traffic benchmarks; Graph WaveNet achieved approximately 0.91–0.93.8 The MultiGCN result is broadly comparable to the upper range of these benchmarks, which is encouraging given that pedestrian flows have a substantially higher coefficient of variation than vehicle traffic (due to event-driven spikes and laneway micro-environments). The per-node bias mechanism appears to be the key enabler, as it allows the model to correct for systematic street-level prediction offsets without requiring additional network capacity.

The relatively modest RMSE (18.99) compared to MAE (4.16) — a ratio of approximately 4.6 — suggests the presence of intermittent large errors on peak-activity streets or event-driven periods. This is consistent with the Melbourne summer calendar: events such as the Australian Open, Melbourne Cup, and New Year's Eve produce street-level pedestrian spikes that are difficult to predict purely from the regular temporal cycle. Future work should incorporate event calendars as additional binary indicator features.

8.2 The Role of Graph Structure

The ablation results confirm that both graph branches contribute non-redundant information. The spatial branch's greater contribution (+11.87 vs. +8.13 for semantic) is consistent with classic urban mobility theory: spatial proximity governs pedestrian route choice, and a street's activity is strongly influenced by its immediate neighbours. However, the semantic branch's substantial contribution indicates that purely proximity-based models systematically under-predict for streets with specific land-use profiles — particularly hospitality-intensive streets, where activity is driven by destination choice rather than through-routing. The dual-branch design effectively separates these two mechanisms.

8.3 Clustering Methodology and the Normalisation Choice

The decision to retain absolute pedestrian flow values in the profile vectors was the most consequential methodological choice in the clustering pipeline. When profiles are row-normalised, every street's activity pattern is expressed as a relative distribution across time blocks, independent of overall activity level. Under Melbourne summer conditions — where the CBD's dominant activity mode is midday and evening-weighted for the majority of streets — this produces a situation where almost all streets appear similar in shape, even if their absolute footfall differs by two orders of magnitude. The GMM then finds no genuine cluster structure (BIC > 0, Silhouette ≈ 0.08).

Retaining raw flow values and applying global standardisation preserves the between-street variance in overall activity level, which is the principal axis along which streets differ for curbside planning purposes. A high-footfall corridor and a peripheral service street have very different reallocation needs; this difference should drive the clustering, not be removed by normalisation.

8.4 Intervention Opportunity Implications

The four-cluster typology maps naturally onto a decision framework for curbside reallocation. The 22 streets classified as Parking Reallocation Priority are candidates for immediate action: their combination of high pedestrian demand and persistently occupied parking indicates a genuine physical bottleneck where pedestrian space is competing with parking for the same kerb. The 185 Morning Pedestrianisation streets have concentrated commute-period demand, suggesting time-of-day flexible curbside regulations (e.g., no parking 7am–10am) would maximise benefit with minimal impact on off-peak parking. The 135 Latent Activation Potential streets present a longer-term planning opportunity: they currently attract low pedestrian flows, but their parking flexibility and land-use context may support activation through pop-up markets, temporary seating, or programming.

The dominance of the Midday–Evening Retail cluster (75.5% of streets) is not a clustering failure but a reflection of Melbourne CBD reality: the majority of arterial streets serve a broadly similar mixed-use retail and hospitality function with moderate and relatively homogeneous pedestrian profiles. The clustering correctly identifies the four interpretable poles of variation from this broad base.

8.5 Limitations

Several limitations of the present work should be acknowledged. First, the study period (149 days, November–March) captures only the Melbourne summer. This is a behaviourally distinctive period — school holidays, major events, and warm weather all influence CBD pedestrian patterns differently from autumn or winter. The absence of winter data means the model may generalise poorly to cooler-month predictions without retraining. Future work should extend the study window to a full calendar year.

Second, imputation of pedestrian flows via XGBoost for 1,329 streets (95.1% of the network) introduces systematic uncertainty. While GroupKFold cross-validation tests cross-street generalisation, the imputation model cannot observe the true pedestrian behaviour on unmonitored streets, and its confidence intervals are not propagated through the GCN–GRU model. A Bayesian or ensemble approach that carries forward imputation uncertainty to final predictions would strengthen the framework's reliability for high-stakes decisions.

Third, the current model produces single-step (t+1) predictions. Curbside planning decisions require reasoning about extended periods (e.g., "what would pedestrian flow look like on this street over a two-hour lunchtime window if parking were removed?"). A multi-step autoregressive or sequence-to-sequence formulation, potentially incorporating intervention counterfactuals as external interventions on the input feature space, is a priority for future development.

Finally, CPU-only training constrains model scale. The hidden dimension of 64 and single-layer GCN design are chosen for training feasibility rather than optimal performance. A GPU-accelerated implementation would permit larger models, wider temporal windows, and multi-step prediction heads.

9. Conclusion

This paper has presented a complete, operationally grounded pipeline for spatio-temporal pedestrian flow modelling and curbside reallocation opportunity identification in Melbourne's Central Business District. The core technical contributions — a dual-branch GCN–GRU architecture with per-node bias, an XGBoost imputation framework for extending sparse pedestrian sensor coverage, and a GMM clustering procedure on raw temporal activity profiles — collectively achieve state-of-the-art predictive performance (test R² = 0.927) while generating interpretable street archetypes that directly support evidence-based planning decisions.

The methodological insights carry implications beyond Melbourne. The finding that row-normalisation of temporal profiles destroys the inter-street variance most relevant for intervention planning is a general caution for urban typology research: the choice of normalisation strategy should reflect the downstream decision objective, not merely statistical convention. Similarly, the dual-graph design — combining geographic and semantic adjacency — offers a template for any city where the functional identity of streets (not just their spatial proximity) is a significant driver of human mobility.

From a planning standpoint, the identification of 22 streets as immediate Parking Reallocation Priority candidates, 185 as candidates for time-of-day pedestrianisation, and 135 as latent activation opportunities provides the City of Melbourne with a quantitative, network-wide evidence base for progressive curbside reallocation — an evidence base that, to the authors' knowledge, does not currently exist at this spatial resolution and temporal granularity.

Future work will focus on three priorities: extending the study window to a full year to capture seasonal variation; developing a multi-step counterfactual prediction capability that models the network-level effects of interventions on neighbouring streets; and implementing GPU-accelerated training to enable larger-scale architectures and ensemble uncertainty quantification.
