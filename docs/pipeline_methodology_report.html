Curbside Intensification

Spatio-temporal pipeline for data-driven curbside reallocation — Melbourne CBD

Methodology, pipeline walkthrough, and experimental comparison  ·  Generated 2026-06-04

This report documents the full analysis pipeline that turns raw parking, pedestrian, land-use and weather data into curbside-reallocation recommendations, and compares the deployed model against two controlled alternatives. Every pipeline step is described twice: once as an ML-engineering operation and once for its real-world meaning on an actual street.

How to read the numbers Two pedestrian error figures appear throughout: all-street MAE (averaged over all 1,397 modelled streets) and sensor-only MAE (averaged over the 74 streets with real pedestrian counters). They measure different populations and must never be compared to each other. The sensor streets are the busiest, highest-variance streets in the city, so their absolute error is naturally much larger. Each figure is labelled wherever it appears.

1 · Purpose & research questions

Urban street design is essentially static, yet how people use streets changes hour by hour and day by day. The curbside — the contested 2–3 metres at the edge of the road — is permanently reserved for vehicles, even when, at certain hours, pedestrians, diners or markets would make far better use of it. Existing practice (NYC Dynamic Curb Management, Seattle Flex Zones) reallocates the curb through manual observation and political feedback, not algorithmic analysis. This project asks whether the decision can be made data-driven.

Research question “How can we reallocate curbside functions based on streets’ temporal behaviour, with the assistance of data-driven methods, using parking and pedestrian activity?”

2 · Pipeline at a glance

The study universe is the Melbourne CBD arterial street network: N = 1,397 streets over T = 14,400 fifteen-minute bins (Nov 2025 – Mar 2026), with F = 23 features per street per bin. Of these, 74 streets carry real pedestrian counters and 143 carry parking sensors; the remainder are filled by a graph-informed imputation model and exist primarily to carry network connectivity.

3 · The pipeline, step by step

01Fetch — raw data ingestion FROZEN

Engineer Idempotent ingestion of heterogeneous sources into data/raw/: Supabase views for parking and pedestrian events, the Open-Meteo hourly archive (fetched in Australia/Melbourne — D-011), fourteen City-of-Melbourne CLUE land-use datasets (cafés, bars, jobs, businesses, floorspace, landmarks), and PTV/bus transit stops. No transformation — a clean, re-runnable raw layer is the contract.
Real world This is the city’s evidence base: who parks where and when, how many people walk past, and what sits around them — the cafés, offices and bars that generate footfall — plus the weather overhead. Nothing is interpreted yet; we are simply gathering every instrument the city already has.

02Snap — sensors & land-use onto streets FROZEN

Engineer A spatial join in projected coordinates (EPSG:3111). Every parking/pedestrian sensor is mapped to its street polygon (nearest-neighbour fallback ≤ 50 m); every CLUE point is assigned to its single nearest arterial within a 250 m catchment as an integer count (interpretable: “11 cafés in this street’s catchment”). A hard arterial filter — str_type ∈ {Arterial, Council Major} — discards minor and private segments so signal is not snapped to streets that later get dropped. Output: static_features.parquet (23 land-use columns).
Real world This turns anonymous sensor IDs and scattered points into street-level facts: “this counter measures Bourke Street; these 11 cafés feed pedestrians onto it.” Restricting to arterials focuses the whole study on the streets where reallocating the curb is actually meaningful — not on driveways and laneway stubs.

03Temporal — time & weather features FROZEN

Engineer Builds the master 15-minute index (14,400 bins). Hourly weather is forward-filled to 15-min resolution; time is encoded cyclically (hour_sin/cos, dow_sin/cos) so that 23:45 and 00:00 are adjacent in feature space rather than maximally distant. Victorian public- and school-holiday flags are attached.
Real world Encodes the context a planner intuits: what time is it, is it a weekday or a holiday, is it raining? A street behaves nothing like itself between 3 a.m. and 1 p.m., or between a normal Tuesday and Melbourne Cup Day — the cyclic encoding lets the model treat the daily and weekly rhythm as the continuous loop it really is.

04Graphs — spatial & semantic adjacency FROZEN

Engineer Two graphs over the 1,397 nodes. Spatial: intersection-topology — polygons that physically touch are connected, weighted by a Gaussian kernel exp(−d/σ) on centroid distance (5,635 directed edges; 25 geometric isolates each get a zero-weight k=1 fallback so every node has a valid row). Semantic: cosine similarity on z-scored log1p land-use vectors, filtered by mutual k-NN so two streets connect only if each is in the other’s top-K (8,097 edges; prevents hub-and-spoke degeneration). Both are symmetrically normalised (D−½AD−½) in Step 08.
Real world Two different meanings of “neighbour.” The spatial graph is where you are — streets that meet at a real intersection, so a change here can flow to there. The semantic graph is what you are — a café-and-bar dining strip resembles another dining strip even on the far side of the city. The model gets to reason along both: physical adjacency and functional similarity.

05Process — parking reconstruction & pedestrian imputation FROZEN

Engineer Two products. (a) Parking occupancy reconstructed from raw events with a time-aware duration cap (D-009). (b) Pedestrian imputation with XGBoost under GroupKFold(5) over streets — the fold split is by street, so cross-validation genuinely measures generalisation to unseen streets, not unseen times. The target is log1p(ped_flow) (heavy right skew); features include a spatial-graph lag (mean flow of sensored neighbours — “what are nearby streets seeing now?”). Three honesty mechanisms: per-street confidence via similarity transfer (D-014); short gaps ≤ 4 bins interpolated while longer runs are flagged as outages, not zero-filled (D-013); non-sensor occupancy set to the sensor mean — “unknown,” not “empty” (D-015).
Real world Only 74 streets have pedestrian counters and 143 have parking sensors, but reallocation decisions are needed everywhere. This step estimates the missing streets from their land use, the time of day, and what their neighbours are measuring. The discipline is what makes it honest: every estimate carries a confidence score, and a broken sensor is recorded as “we don’t know,” never as “nobody walked here” — because a fake zero would manufacture a fake opportunity to pedestrianise.

06Aggregate — weekly temporal profiles FROZEN

Engineer Collapses the 14,400-bin series into a compact weekly fingerprint per street: 42 parking + 42 pedestrian features (7 days × 6 time-blocks). Pedestrian profiles are row-normalised by each street’s own mean flow, so the clustering downstream compares shape (when a street is busy) rather than scale (how busy) — putting a quiet lane and a busy boulevard on equal footing.
Real world A signature of how each street “breathes” across a typical week — the morning commuter pulse, the lunch spike, the evening dining surge, the weekend drift. This is the object a human planner carries in their head; here it becomes a vector.

07Cluster — behavioural archetypes & flexibility windows FROZEN

Engineer StandardScaler → PCA(20 comps, 99.5% variance) → Gaussian Mixture, with a BIC sweep over k = 2…10 selecting the true minimum at k = 4. Stability is checked by bootstrap ARI (0.888) and silhouette (0.526). Each cluster is labelled by its parking centroid peak; flexibility windows are time-blocks where pedestrian demand is high and parking occupancy is low.
Real world Sorts the city into a handful of street “personalities” and — directly answering sub-RQ 1 — flags the moments when a street is full of people but its parking sits empty: the prime candidates for handing the curb to pedestrians. 1,109 streets show at least one such window.

08Cube — the model’s input substrate FROZEN

Engineer Assembles the dense float32 tensor [N=1,397 × T=14,400 × F=23] in node order, builds the two symmetrically-normalised sparse adjacencies, and computes norm_stats — with log1p applied to ped_flow before z-scoring (D-012), which collapses the heavy z = 4–10 tail of raw counts. Also emits ped_valid_mask [N×T] so training can ignore sensor outages. The cube is stored raw and normalised on the fly, so the log-transform contract lives in a single pair of helper functions.
Real world Everything the city knows — footfall, parking, clock, calendar, weather, land use — stacked into one aligned block of space × time × feature. This is the unified “world” the learning model will read; every earlier step exists to fill one slice of it correctly.

09Train — dual-head MultiGCN FROZEN

Engineer At each of the 96 timesteps in a 24-hour window, two GCN branches (spatial + semantic) map F → H = 64; their concatenation feeds a 2-layer GRU that compresses the window to a per-node hidden state, which two linear heads turn into next-step ped_flow and occupancy_rate (each with a per-node bias). Joint loss MAE_ped + 0.5 · masked_MAE_park; parking supervision is masked to the 143 sensor streets. Chronological 70/15/15 split (no leakage), Adam 1e-3, ReduceLROnPlateau, early stop on val ped MAE. 68,076 parameters.
Real world The model learns to answer: “given the last 24 hours on this street and its neighbours, what happens in the next 15 minutes?” The spatial branch passes influence between connected streets, the semantic branch between similar streets, and the GRU remembers the daily rhythm. It predicts people and parking jointly because curbside decisions trade one against the other.

10Interpret — what drives the prediction

Engineer Permutation importance: each of the 23 feature columns is replaced with noise in the validation set and the MAE increase is measured (3 repeats, averaged). Branch attribution zeroes each GCN branch in turn. Baseline MAE 5.03.
Real world Opens the black box and answers sub-RQ 2. Pedestrian flow is overwhelmingly driven by its own recent momentum and by how trustworthy the local data is — not by the weather. The physical-adjacency graph matters about three times as much as the functional-similarity graph: where a street is beats what it is.

11Scenario — counterfactual intervention simulation

Engineer An autoregressive rollout runs two futures from the same starting window — a clean baseline and a treated one — and reports their difference per street per step. Three interventions: pedestrianise (occupancy → 0), restrict_park (occupancy → m), boost_ped (+raw uplift). Network effects are characterised by the joint parking head (learned spillover), analytical graph diffusion Ak (k = 1…3 hops), semantic-neighbour reporting, confidence-weighted ranking, and rebound half-life. An honesty gate (D-017) refuses, by default, to run on a street that lacks a real sensor for the signal being perturbed.
Real world Answers sub-RQ 3: “if I pedestrianise this block for four hours, what happens here — and to the streets around it?” It lets a planner test an intervention before touching the real curb, and see the knock-on effects ripple outward through the network. The honesty gate enforces the project’s core principle: quantitative claims are made only where the perturbed quantity is actually measured.

12Export — interactive planner tool

Engineer Enriches streets_viz.geojson with opportunity scores, predictions and scenario outputs, and writes the summary JSONs consumed by the Mapbox-GL frontend and the Flask scenario API.
Real world Turns the whole analysis into something a planner can use: an interactive map where you click a street, see its archetype and flexibility windows, and run a live what-if scenario.

4 · The model: dual-head MultiGCN

Input  [batch, W=96, N=1397, F=23]
  ├─ GCN branch (spatial graph)   F → 64   ─┐
  ├─ GCN branch (semantic graph)  F → 64   ─┤ concat → [batch, W, N, 128]
  │                                          └─▶ GRU (2 layers, dropout 0.1) → [batch, N, 64]
  │                                                 ├─ head_ped   → next-step ped_flow      (+per-node bias)
  │                                                 └─ head_park  → next-step occupancy_rate (+per-node bias)
Loss = MAE_ped  +  0.5 · masked_MAE_park        (parking loss masked to 143 sensor streets)
Parameters = 68,076

The architecture is deliberately small. The point of the thesis is not raw predictive power but a faithful, interpretable spatio-temporal model whose graph structure can be probed (Step 10) and counterfactually perturbed (Step 11). Two GCN views + a GRU is the minimal design that is genuinely spatio-temporal: graph message-passing for space, recurrence for time.

5 · Experiments compared

To isolate why the pipeline works, three models were trained and evaluated on the same Category-A cube, with pedestrian accuracy measured on the 74 real sensor streets. ExpA and ExpB were each run across 3 seeds (42, 1, 2) and are reported as mean ± std, so the comparison survives random-seed noise.

Lower MAE is better. Sensor-MAE and all-street-MAE are different populations — read down each column, never across. ExpA’s —: an all-street figure is undefined, since ExpA contains only the 74 sensor nodes.

Finding 1 — network connectivity is the dominant lever

Restricting the graph to only the sensor streets (ExpA, 54.71) is more than twice as bad as keeping the full graph (ExpB, 24.97). The gap (~30 MAE) dwarfs the seed-to-seed std (≈ 1.3 and 0.3), so it is not noise. The mechanism is visible in the data: deleting the non-sensor streets leaves 35 of the 74 sensor nodes completely isolated.

Why Sensors are sparse in space — two sensor streets are almost never physically adjacent. In the real road network they connect to each other through the unsensored streets in between. Cutting those out does not merely remove context; it severs the bridges that link the sensors, and the spatial branch collapses for half of them. This is the empirical answer to “do the streets in between matter?” — emphatically yes.

Finding 2 — loss masking is a separate, smaller lever

ExpB (masked, 24.97) beats the deployed model on sensor streets (28.02) by ~3 MAE. Both use the identical full graph, so this gain is not connectivity — it comes from not training on the imputed pedestrian targets, which are themselves estimates (median imputation R² ≈ 0.18, near noise). Masking stops the model from learning to reproduce noisy guesses on 1,323 streets and lets it concentrate on the real signal.

Don’t merge the two levers Connectivity (the big win) comes from including the full graph; clean supervision (the small win) comes from the loss mask. They are independent: the unmasked deployed model also has the full graph, which is why it sits at 28, not 55.

The same architecture and data yield two regimes depending only on where the loss is computed. The deployed model is the right choice for the product — scenario simulation needs a plausible value on every street for the network to propagate through — while ExpB demonstrates the accuracy ceiling on the streets we can actually verify. Reporting both, honestly, is the point.

Comparability guardrails
  • Old vs new ablation are not comparable. An earlier ablation on the pre-Category-A cube (with linear de-normalisation) reported ExpA ≈ ExpB ≈ 22. That run used a different dataset and a different inverse transform; only the within-this-run ranking above is valid. One cannot claim “ExpA went from 22 to 55.”
  • Magnitude is scale-sensitive, direction is certain. The expm1 inverse of the log1p target amplifies errors on high-count streets, so the precise 2.2× ratio is softer than it looks; the ranking (full graph ≫ sensor-only) holds across every seed.
  • ExpA is unstable as well as worse. One ExpA seed collapsed to validation R² ≈ 0.07 — the sensor-only model is erratic, not merely less accurate.

Known limitations (carried as thesis caveats)

  • Imputation is for coverage, not sensor accuracy. The ablation shows the imputed streets exist to carry network connectivity; the verifiable accuracy ceiling is set by the sensor streets.
  • Parking-head extrapolation. 1,254 non-sensor streets have no parking supervision — their occupancy predictions are extrapolations.
  • Autoregressive horizon. Scenario rollouts beyond ~4 hours (16 steps) are indicative, not precise — prediction error compounds.
  • Confidence honesty. Median per-street imputation R² ≈ 0.18; only 2 streets earn the high-confidence tier. This is reported, not hidden.
  • Study window. Nov 2025 – Mar 2026 (southern-hemisphere summer/autumn); seasonal generalisation is untested.
